How weighted GPA works

A weighted GPA gives each course influence in proportion to its credit hours. Multiply every grade point by its credits, add those products, then divide by the total credits. If one course has twice the credits of another, it carries twice the weight in this calculation.

Example: A 3-credit course at 3.6 and a 1-credit course at 4.0 produce (3 x 3.6 + 1 x 4.0) / 4 = 3.7. This arithmetic does not define the grade-point scale; your institution does.

Attendance math, explained

Attendance percentage is the classes attended divided by classes conducted, multiplied by 100. To recover to a target by attending every upcoming class, each attended class increases both your attended count and total class count.

If you have attended 18 of 24 classes, you are at 75%. To reach 80%, solve (18 + x) / (24 + x) = 0.80; attending 6 classes in a row reaches the target exactly. Check your institution's attendance policy for how it counts sessions.

Calculate a final-exam target

When internal work and the final exam have different weights, combine them as a weighted score. If internal performance is 80% and contributes 40% of the course, it contributes 32 percentage points to the overall result.

For a 70% overall target with a final worth 60%, you need (70 - 32) / 0.60 = 63.34% on the final. This assumes both component scores use percentages and the weights sum to 100%; use your course's actual assessment structure.

Choose a grade scale carefully

A grade calculator can only return a meaningful grade when its thresholds match your course. Some institutions use letter grades, grade points, or subject-specific assessment rules. Copy the boundaries from a current official source before using an estimate.

Split study time realistically

Dividing time evenly is a useful starting point, not a complete timetable. Difficult topics may need more time, and breaks should be planned outside focused study blocks. Revisit the split after a short session and adjust based on progress.

What a percentage can tell you

Divide marks earned by the maximum marks and multiply by 100. Percentages help compare results across tests with different maximum scores, but they do not account for different difficulty or grading policies.
