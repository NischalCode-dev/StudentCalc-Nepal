Study planning

Make a study plan you can actually follow

A useful study plan turns a deadline into manageable tasks while leaving room to practise, review, and rest. The right plan is personal: a timetable for SEE preparation, NEB subjects, or university exams should reflect your syllabus, available hours, current understanding, and other responsibilities—not just divide every subject equally.

A practical planning method

  1. List exam dates, assignment deadlines, and the topics or skills still to cover.
  2. Break broad topics into actions you can finish in one focused session, such as solving a set of problems or revising one chapter.
  3. Prioritize by deadline, importance, and how much practice you need. Schedule difficult work when you can focus.
  4. Include active recall, practice questions, and spaced review instead of only rereading notes.
  5. Plan breaks and sleep, then leave some unallocated time for work that takes longer than expected.
  6. At the end of a block, record what you completed and adjust the next plan based on evidence.

Using a simple time split

The StudentCalc study-time tool divides available minutes evenly: minutes per subject = floor(available study minutes / number of subjects). Any remainder is left unallocated. This is an arithmetic starting point only; the calculator does not prioritize topics or include breaks.

Example 1: a short revision block

  1. You have 125 minutes for three subjects.
  2. 125 / 3 = 41 whole minutes per subject, with 125 - (41 × 3) = 2 minutes unallocated.
  3. Use the split as a first draft, then move time toward the topic with the nearest deadline or most practice needs.

Example 2: an evening study session

  1. You set aside 180 focused minutes across four subjects.
  2. 180 / 4 = 45 minutes each, with no remainder.
  3. If one subject needs extra problem practice, adjust the blocks—for example, 60, 45, 40, and 35 minutes still total 180.

Plan around your real calendar

Students in Nepal may be balancing school or college, tuition, travel, family responsibilities, and exam schedules that can change. Check the latest exam notice from your school or examination authority, and use your actual available time rather than planning every waking hour. A realistic plan with consistent practice is easier to adjust than a packed timetable that leaves no room for rest or unexpected tasks.

Common planning mistakes

  • Dividing time equally even when subjects need different amounts of practice.
  • Scheduling every minute without breaks, meals, sleep, or a buffer.
  • Writing vague tasks such as “study science” instead of a specific chapter or activity.
  • Using an old exam date or timetable without checking for official updates.
  • Measuring study only by hours rather than by completed practice and understanding.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a study block be?

There is no single best duration. Choose a focused block that fits the task and your attention, then take a break when needed. Adjust based on what helps you learn.

Should every subject get equal time?

Not necessarily. An equal split is convenient for a first estimate, but deadlines, difficulty, and your confidence should shape the final plan.

Does the study-time calculator include breaks?

No. Enter the minutes you intend to use for study. Schedule breaks separately and include them in your calendar.
